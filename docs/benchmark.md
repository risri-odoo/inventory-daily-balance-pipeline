## Optimization measured: incremental recompute vs. full rebuild

After all batches were loaded, one small late batch (50 receipts dated 2026-03-25, 5 days before the horizon) was loaded into fresh copies of the database, 3 times per mode. Medians:

| mode | pairs rebuilt | balance rows written | rebuild stage ms | whole run ms |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| incremental (default) | 50 | 300 | 288 | 333 |
| `--full-rebuild` | 500 | 15,000 | 919 | 956 |

The incremental rebuild stage was **3.2x faster** (2.9x for the whole run) and both modes produced identical balance tables: **True**.

Why: a full rebuild reads the whole current ledger and deletes and rewrites every row of every pair. The incremental rebuild reads the ledger only for the affected pairs, rewrites them from their earliest affected date to the horizon, keeps all earlier days, and extends other pairs (if the horizon moves) by carrying their last closing forward.

### Before vs. after narrowing the incremental read

The first benchmark run (2026-11-08T09:49:00Z, raw results in [evidence/benchmark_baseline.json](evidence/benchmark_baseline.json)) showed the incremental rebuild was barely faster than a full rebuild: both read the **whole** current ledger through `v_movement_leg`, and that read was most of the cost. Two changes followed:

1. `v_movement_current` was rewritten from a `GROUP BY` join to `NOT EXISTS (a higher version)`: one primary-key lookup per row, and SQLite can push filters into the view.
2. The incremental rebuild reads legs only for the affected pairs (a temporary table plus two new indexes on `(sku, location_id)` and `(sku, to_location_id)`). Unaffected pairs are extended by carrying their last closing forward, without reading the ledger.

## Where the time goes and next steps

- **Validate + load** is row-by-row Python: each row runs the validation rules and two primary-key lookups (duplicate check and current version) before its insert. That is simple to explain and fast enough here. Next step: look keys up in bulk (one query per batch into a dict) and insert with `executemany`.
- **Rebuild** reads every leg of each affected pair, from its first movement, although only the days from the earliest affected date are rewritten. Next step: also filter the read by date and start from the stored closing of the day before; or keep a materialised current-version table.
- **Bigger data**: the same SQL runs on DuckDB (columnar, parallel) with few changes, and Polars could do the dense-grid build as vectorised window operations. For years of history, partition balances by month so a backdated correction only rewrites the partitions from its date onwards.
- SQLite is single-writer, so batches are processed one at a time. That matches the ordering rules (filename order, stop at the first failure) but caps throughput at one core.
